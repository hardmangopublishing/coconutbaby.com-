Bath time

How often, what to use, what to skip

Most babies are bathed more often than they need to be, with more product than they need, and the bath aisle is designed to keep it that way. Here is the short version.

Who this is for

  • New parents working out how often to bathe a newborn and what, if anything, to put in the water.
  • Anyone whose baby has dry skin and suspects the bath routine is part of the cause. It often is.
  • Parents standing in front of a shelf of tear-free, gentle, naturally-derived bottles wanting to know which words mean something.

Who should skip it

  • Anyone treating a skin condition. Eczema and dermatitis come with a bathing protocol from your pediatrician, and it overrides everything here.
  • Parents shopping for bubble bath for an infant. See below — this is the one product I would actively steer you away from.
  • Anyone buying a bath seat or ring as a safety device. They are convenience items and they have been involved in drownings.

Less often than you think

Newborns do not get dirty. They are not crawling on floors, they are not sweating through workouts, and their skin barrier is still developing. Bathing two or three times a week is enough for most babies in the early months, with a clean-up of the face, neck folds and diaper area on the other days.

Until the umbilical cord stump falls off, the standard advice is sponge baths rather than immersion — a warm damp cloth, a warm room, and no submerging. Your pediatrician will tell you when to switch, and it is usually a matter of a week or two.

The reason this matters beyond convenience is that water and cleanser both strip the skin's natural oils. Daily bathing with soap is one of the most common causes of the dry, flaky baby skin that then sends parents out to buy lotion to fix it. Bathing less is free and it works.

Once a baby is crawling, eating solids and getting genuinely grubby, daily baths become reasonable — and by then their skin has matured enough to handle it. Bath time also becomes a useful part of a bedtime routine, which is a legitimate reason to do it more often than hygiene strictly requires. Just keep the cleanser light.

What belongs in the water

For a newborn: plain warm water. That is genuinely sufficient for the first weeks, and many pediatricians recommend exactly that.

After that, a mild fragrance-free cleanser used sparingly, on the parts that need it, rather than a full-body lather every time. The neck folds, the armpits, behind the ears and the diaper area are where a baby actually needs cleaning; the rest is mostly rinsing.

You will see coconut-derived surfactants throughout this category, and it is worth knowing what they are. Cocamidopropyl betaine, coco-glucoside and sodium cocoyl isethionate are all cleansing agents made from coconut oil, and they are the gentle alternative to harsher detergents. They are a good sign on a label rather than a bad one — though cocamidopropyl betaine is a recognized contact allergen for a small number of people, so if a wash seems to be causing irritation, that is a reasonable ingredient to suspect.

"Tear-free" is a formulation claim about pH and surfactant choice, not a legal standard. It is a genuine feature and not a guarantee. Rinse carefully around the eyes regardless.

What to leave on the shelf

Bubble bath. The one I would actively avoid for infants and young children. Sitting in surfactant-laden water for twenty minutes is irritating to sensitive skin, and it has a long-standing association with urinary tract irritation, particularly in girls. It is a product designed to sell to parents rather than to serve babies.

Adult soap and shampoo. Formulated for a different pH and a mature skin barrier.

Essential oils in the water. Concentrated, chemically active, and several are not considered appropriate for infants. A calming bath does not require lavender oil.

Talc-based powder. Inhalation risk, and it has been replaced by better options for every job it was used for.

The rules that outrank every product

Never leave a baby alone in the bath. Not for a second, not for the door, not for the phone. Babies can drown in a very small amount of water and it happens silently. If you have to leave the room, the baby comes with you, wrapped in a towel. This is the single most important line on this page.

Check the water with your wrist or elbow, not your hand, which is less sensitive to heat. Warm, not hot. Run cold water last so the tap itself is not scalding, and consider turning your water heater's maximum temperature down — it is a one-time adjustment that protects for years.

Bath seats and rings are not safety devices. They tip, and they create the illusion that a baby is secure enough for you to look away. Use them as a convenience if you like, with exactly the same supervision you would give without one.

Which tub, and when to stop using one

A dedicated infant tub is genuinely useful for the first months, because supporting a slippery newborn in a full-size bath is awkward and tiring. What matters is a shape that holds the baby at a slight incline with their head clear of the water, a non-slip base, and a size that fits wherever you plan to use it — many parents end up bathing in the kitchen sink for weeks, which saves a great deal of bending.

Skip the ones with built-in thermometers as a reason to buy; check the water yourself regardless. Skip anything with a foam insert that stays damp, which is how mold gets into a baby's bath.

Most babies move to the big bath once they can sit steadily and reliably, usually somewhere around six months. Use only a few inches of water, put a non-slip mat down, and apply exactly the same never-look-away rule.

The two minutes after

This is where the bath either helps or hurts the skin, and it costs nothing to get right.

Lift the baby out, wrap immediately — babies lose heat fast — and pat dry rather than rub. Rubbing a towel over newborn skin is abrasive and pointless.

Then moisturize while the skin is still slightly damp, within a few minutes. Any emollient works better applied to damp skin because there is still water present for it to seal in, and this is exactly the moment the plain jar of coconut oil earns its place.

Pay attention to the folds — under the neck, behind the knees, in the armpits, around the thighs. Those get damp, stay damp, and are where irritation starts. Dry them properly.
