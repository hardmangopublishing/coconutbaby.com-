Guide two

Balms, lotions and diaper creams

A coconut on the front of a bottle is a design decision. The ingredient list on the back is the product. Here is how to read one in about fifteen seconds.

Who this is for

  • Parents who want a finished product rather than a jar of plain oil — something that spreads easily, absorbs faster, and does not leave a film on everything.
  • Anyone standing in front of forty bottles that all say gentle, natural and pediatrician-recommended, trying to work out which claim means something.
  • Parents who need a diaper cream and want to know why the good ones look so unglamorous.

Who should skip it

  • Anyone whose baby is doing fine on plain coconut oil. A more complicated product is not an upgrade.
  • Parents treating diagnosed eczema, dermatitis or a persistent rash. Those come with a pediatrician's plan, and an off-the-shelf balm is not part of it unless they say so.
  • Anyone shopping by fragrance. Fragrance is the single most common irritant in this category and the most common reason a gentle product is not.

Read the list back-to-front

Ingredients are listed in descending order of quantity. That single rule does most of the work.

Read the first three. In most lotions the first ingredient is water, which is fine and normal — a lotion is oil and water held together — but it tells you what you are buying by volume. If the coconut on the front is the tenth ingredient, appearing after the preservative and two fragrances, the coconut is a picture rather than a feature.

Then read the last three. Preservatives and fragrance live down there, and in a product for a newborn those are the lines worth a second of attention. A preservative in a water-containing product is not a villain — a lotion without one grows mould — but fragrance is optional, common, and the ingredient most often behind a reaction.

Then count. A balm can reasonably be four ingredients. If a lotion needs twenty-six, ask what the other twenty-two are doing. Sometimes the honest answer is texture and shelf stability, which are real jobs. Often the answer is that a longer list looks more like science.

What the words on the front actually promise

Natural — nothing. There is no legal definition governing it in cosmetics. Poison ivy is natural.

Hypoallergenic — nearly nothing. It means the manufacturer believes the product is less likely to cause a reaction. No standard test sits behind the word, and no agency verifies it before it goes on the bottle.

Dermatologist tested — that a dermatologist tested it. Not that they liked it, and not what the result was.

Pediatrician recommended — that some pediatricians recommend it. Usually true and usually uninformative.

Fragrance-free — meaningful and worth looking for. Note that unscented is a different word: an unscented product may contain masking fragrance to cover the smell of its own ingredients.

USDA Organic — meaningful. It is a certification with an auditable standard and a body enforcing it. Whether an organic lotion is better for your baby than a non-organic one is a separate question, but the seal itself is not empty.

National Eczema Association Seal of Acceptance — meaningful in a narrow way. It indicates the product was reviewed against that organisation's criteria for sensitive skin. It is not a treatment endorsement.

The four ingredients parents ask about

Essential oils. The one worth real caution. They are marketed as the natural alternative to fragrance, and they are still fragrance — concentrated, chemically active, and capable of irritating skin. Several common ones are not considered appropriate for infants at all. If a product for a newborn is scented with essential oils, that is a reason to look harder, not a reassurance.

Phenoxyethanol and parabens. Preservatives, and the subject of more internet argument than evidence. Both are permitted at limited concentrations. The practical point is that a water-based product must be preserved somehow, and an unpreserved lotion in a warm bathroom is its own hazard. If you would rather avoid the debate, buy a balm — no water, little need for preservative.

Mineral oil and petrolatum. Unfashionable and effective. Cosmetic-grade petrolatum is one of the best-studied barrier ingredients there is. It is not natural, which is the entire case against it.

Aloe, calendula, shea. Common, generally well tolerated, and doing broadly the same emollient job as the coconut oil already in the tub.

Balm, lotion, cream — the difference is water

A balm or ointment is mostly oils and waxes with little or no water. It is thick, it sits on the surface, it needs few or no preservatives, and it is the best barrier of the three. It is also greasy and hard to spread on a squirming baby.

A lotion is mostly water with oil emulsified into it. It spreads beautifully and absorbs quickly, which is why people like it, and it is the least occlusive of the three. It needs preservatives.

A cream sits between the two. For most everyday nursery use it is the sensible middle.

None of these is better in the abstract. Thick barrier for a diaper area or a wind-chapped face in January; light lotion for after a bath in August.

Diaper cream is a different product entirely

Everything above concerns moisturising. Diaper cream is a barrier, and its job is to keep moisture off the skin rather than in it. That is why the effective ones are unfashionable pastes rather than elegant lotions, and why coconut oil alone is a weak substitute — it is not thick enough to hold a barrier under a wet diaper for hours.

The workhorse ingredient is zinc oxide, and the practical spec is its percentage, printed on the tube. Lower concentrations are for everyday prevention at each change; higher ones are for skin already irritated. Some products pair zinc oxide with coconut oil, which is a reasonable combination — the zinc does the barrier work, the oil improves how it spreads.

When the tube is the wrong tool

A diaper rash that is bright red with small satellite spots at its edges, one that has lasted more than a few days despite consistent barrier cream, one that is blistering, weeping or bleeding, or any rash with a fever — none of these are shopping problems. Yeast, bacterial infection and other conditions look similar to ordinary irritation to almost everyone who is not a doctor, and they need different treatment.

Do not apply steroid creams left over from an adult, and do not use talc-based powders.
