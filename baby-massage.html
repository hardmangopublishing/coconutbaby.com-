Baby massage

The one thing coconut oil is genuinely best at

Not a treatment, not a sleep hack, and not something you need a course or a kit for. Twenty minutes, a warm room, and a baby who is in the mood.

Who this is for

  • Parents wanting unhurried physical time with a baby that is not feeding, changing or settling.
  • Partners who are not breastfeeding and are looking for their own version of that closeness. This is the classic answer, and it is a good one.
  • Anyone who bought a jar of coconut oil and wants to use it for the thing it is actually best at.

Who should skip it

  • Anyone with a baby who is unwell, feverish, or recently vaccinated at the injection site. Wait, and ask if unsure.
  • Parents of premature or medically complex babies, until your pediatric team has said yes. They may well say yes, and they may have specific guidance.
  • Anyone hoping it will fix colic or make a baby sleep through. It may soothe. It is not a treatment, and going in with that expectation turns a nice thing into a disappointment.

What it is actually for

Infant massage is old, widespread, and studied with the usual caveats: research suggests benefits around calming, bonding, and in some settings weight gain in preterm infants, and much of that research is small. Treat the evidence as encouraging rather than conclusive.

The honest case does not need the studies. It is twenty minutes of undivided, unhurried physical attention where nobody is trying to get anything done. You learn your baby's signals — what a settled body looks like, what an overstimulated one looks like — and they learn that touch is not always a precursor to being wiped, dressed or put down. That is worth doing whether or not it moves any measurable number.

It is also, practically, the calmest part of many parents' day, and that counts for something on both sides of the towel.

Choosing an oil

Coconut oil is the standard recommendation and deserves to be. It has genuine slip, it is edible so a hand in a mouth is a non-event, it costs almost nothing, and its ingredient list is one line long. Virgin has a strong coconut smell; refined is close to odorless if you or the baby would rather.

Fractionated coconut oil, sold as MCT oil, stays liquid and leaves less residue, which is why massage therapists like it. For a nursery it is a convenience rather than an upgrade.

Patch test before the first full massage, even if the oil is already in your kitchen. A small amount on the inside of a forearm, then wait a day and look for redness or bumps.

Two things to avoid. Nut-derived oils if there is any nut allergy in the family — and remember coconut is classified as a tree nut for labeling purposes in the United States, so raise it with your pediatrician if that applies. And any oil containing essential oils or fragrance, which are common in products sold specifically as "baby massage oil" and are the least appropriate ingredient to rub across an infant's whole body.

How to actually do it

There is no correct sequence, and you do not need a class — though they exist, they are enjoyable, and some are free through local health services.

Pick the moment. Not hungry, not exhausted, not immediately after a feed. The sweet spot is a baby who is awake, calm and alert. After a bath is traditional and works well because they are already undressed and warm.

Warm the room, then your hands. Babies lose heat fast when undressed. Warm the oil by rubbing it between your palms rather than heating it.

Ask. This sounds precious and it is not — the standard practice is to rest your hands on the baby, catch their eye, and say something consistent like "massage time." Over weeks it becomes a signal they recognize, and it builds the idea that this is something done with them rather than to them.

Start with the legs and feet. Least intrusive, easiest to reach, and a good place to see whether they are enjoying it. Then arms, chest, back. Slow, firm-but-gentle strokes — a feather touch tickles and irritates. Skip the face.

Watch for the cues to stop, which is the actual skill here. Turning the head away, arching, splaying fingers, going stiff, fussing, or hiccupping all mean enough. Enough might be four minutes. That is a successful massage, not a failed one.

A slippery baby is a real hazard

Massage on the floor. A towel on the carpet, not a bed, not a changing table, not a sofa. An oiled baby is genuinely hard to hold and this is the most likely way for something to go wrong. On the floor, there is nowhere to fall.

Keep oil out of the bath. If you bathe after massaging, the tub becomes slippery for both of you. Massage after the bath instead, which is better for the skin anyway.

Nothing on broken skin, and nothing over a rash you have not identified. If something is going on with the skin, that is a conversation with your pediatrician before it is a massage.

When to start, and how it changes

Most guidance suggests waiting a few weeks after birth before starting full massage, partly so the umbilical area is fully healed and partly because very new babies are easily overstimulated. Gentle stroking through a blanket is fine from the beginning. Ask your pediatrician if your baby arrived early or had any complications.

The early months are the easy ones. A baby who cannot yet roll will lie contentedly through a long session, and this is when the twenty-minute version happens.

Then they start moving, and it changes completely. A six-month-old rolls away mid-stroke; a one-year-old treats the whole thing as a game. This is not the end of it — it just becomes shorter and more opportunistic. A few minutes on the legs after a bath, a back rub at bedtime. The ritual survives even when the format does not, and that was always the part that mattered.

What you actually need

A towel, a jar of oil, and a warm room. That is the whole list.

What is sold as a baby massage kit — the branded oil, the mat, the instructional cards — is a bundle of things you have or do not need. If you want one purchase beyond the oil, make it a couple of large soft towels, because oil goes through a thin one and onto whatever is underneath.

Cotton muslin squares are the other genuinely useful item, for the same reason and about a dozen others. You will not regret owning too many.
